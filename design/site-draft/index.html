How it works

  1. Write a brief.

    Say what the robot is and where it works. It asks only what it cannot work out.

  2. It builds the worlds.

    An agent reads your brief and decides how many worlds to build, and why.

  3. It runs them and finds failures.

    The same robot runs in every world. Failures group by type, down to a line of code.

  4. What it can't fix goes to a bounty.

    It lists what it did not test. You can offer that work to outside testers.

Read these with care. A separate audit of the simulator found that one wind term and two judge rules shape the counts. Coming off the glass relied on wind blowing out of a solid wall. Running out of time comes from the rule that pauses at 10 m/s. A threshold sets the count for saying done.

What held up

The audit called one finding fair. The low-pressure guard goes blind once fan speed passes 0.896. Each of the 9 times the robot came off the glass, the estimate read safe while true pressure sat under the limit.

Better sensing alone did not solve it. Given true pressure in 29 re-runs, the robot came off 8 times, not 9.

What the AI fix did

One change: suction while cleaning, from 0.85 to 0.9. We tested it on 104 new worlds.

Of the 104, 6 worlds went from failed to passed. One went from stalled to a battery stop, which is not a pass. One moved from a slide to a stall. None got worse.

The patch also removes the low-pressure guard while cleaning, so we have not applied it. A live AI try on a small check run did not help: 0 better, 0 worse.

What needs a person

In 5 of those 9, the robot had already stopped with suction at full. The controller had no more force to ask for.

That is a decision about suction, tracks or tether, or a limit on working wind. So it goes to its owner or to a bounty. An earlier reaction by the controller is not ruled out.

What it can't fix goes to a bounty.

You set the limits and fund it. Testers send a world that breaks your robot. A contract is written to pay a fixed amount for each new, valid failure.

Not live yet. The gate runs on one machine and writes to a local ledger in the app. It sends nothing on chain. The gate cannot prove a world is fair: your limits and a human arbiter do most of that work.

Left out of the Dubai run

  • Airborne sand. No physics for sand hitting sensors, pads or the fan. We test only dust on the glass.
  • Wind by height. Wind does not grow with height here. We mimic a tall tower with high wind and a long tether.
  • Sun and glare. We test heat as air and battery temperature only.
  • Rain, ice, snow and salt. No physics for these. Wet glass only approximates moisture.
  • Real hardware. Only the simulated robot ran. No real motors, sensors or tether wear.

The gate judges only worlds the simulator can run. A gap with no physics stays on this list until someone models it.

What a tester's world has to get through

  1. Inside your limits. The world file is numbers only, and each one stays in the range you set.
  2. Possible for the hardware. If wind beats the fan's full grip on clean glass, no controller could hold on. The gate rejects it.
  3. It fails. The exact world must break the robot. A pass or a safe hold ends the claim here.
  4. It is new. A failure already paid for in the same conditions does not pay twice.
  5. It repeats. Eight re-runs, each with a small hidden change. At least seven must end in the same failure.
  6. An easier twin passes. The same world, moved most of the way back to normal, must pass.
  7. Time to object. An arbiter can reject it during a dispute window. Then the winner collects.

Seven of eight means it repeated on those draws. A world that fails only 70% of the time still clears this about one time in four, so the check is not proof.

What this is not

Not a real robot.

A simulated robot on hand-built physics, not checked against real hardware.

Not a reliability score.

The counts describe one set of hard worlds. Choose other worlds and they change.

Not a model of sand in the air.

The brief says sandstorms. We test dust on the glass and seal, plus wind. Airborne sand has no physics here.

Not untouched numbers.

Some tuning came after results, and git cannot confirm the order. Physics after a robot comes off the glass is unreliable, so we quote nothing from it.

Not proven on every machine.

You can replay the recorded run. Replay across machines is unknown.

Not footage of a robot.

The film is a re-render of recorded physics. Sky, city, glass and camera are art.
